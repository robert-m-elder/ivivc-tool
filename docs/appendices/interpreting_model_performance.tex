\section{How to Interpret Model Performance}
\label{app:model-performance}

No single metric determines whether an IVIVC model is meaningful. An evaluation of model performance can include goodness of fit, prediction error, model complexity, cross-validation behavior, residual patterns, parameter uncertainty, and whether the fitted relationship is scientifically plausible for the material, degradation mechanism, and intended use. Performance metrics are descriptive and comparative tools, not acceptance criteria. Small datasets can produce unstable estimates, and similar metric values do not necessarily indicate equivalent scientific validity. For analyses intended to support regulatory submissions, the depth of supporting evidence depends on the model's intended use and applicable credibility considerations \cite{fda_modeling_guidance,fda_reporting_guidance}.

The app reports metrics and comparison tables as descriptive aids for user review; the scope and advisory status described in the Introduction apply throughout this appendix. Users are responsible for determining how to document preprocessing assumptions, fitting settings, model-selection rationale, limitations, and any planned use of the model outside the fitted data range, consistent with the intended use and applicable credibility considerations.

\subsection{Measurement uncertainty and weighted fitting}
\label{app:measurement-uncertainty}

For Approach 2, supplied SD or SEM represents the standard uncertainty $\sigma_i$ of the response entered at row $i$. SD describes variation among individual measurements; SEM describes uncertainty in a reported mean. These quantities differ from a fitted parameter's standard error, which is calculated from the parameter covariance matrix. A mean is one fitted observation, even if it summarizes several replicates. Optional replicate counts are metadata: they do not increase the fitted sample size or divide an entered SEM again. Input choices are described in Section~\ref{sec:measurement-uncertainty}.

With supplied errors, the app minimizes squared residuals relative to their SD/SEM:
\[
Q=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{r_i}{\sigma_i}\right)^2,
\qquad r_i=y_i-f(t_i;\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}),
\qquad w_i=\frac{1}{\sigma_i^2}.
\]
Here $r_i$ is observed minus fitted response and $w_i$ is an observation weight. The responses and errors use the analyzed scale after preprocessing. Smaller errors give an observation more influence. The same errors are used in the starting-value search, the full fit, and the corresponding CV training fits. This is weighted least squares \cite{nistWeightedLeastSquares}.

The app treats the supplied error scale as absolute: the fitted covariance is not rescaled to match the residual scatter. Without supplied errors, each calibration source retains ordinary least squares and a covariance scale estimated from its residuals \cite{scipyCurveFit}. Equal constant errors give the ordinary least-squares objective up to a constant factor, but the covariance conventions can still differ. A perfect fit need not have zero parameter uncertainty when positive SD/SEM is supplied.

Supplied errors are treated as given, even when they were estimated from few replicates. The app does not propagate uncertainty in those error estimates. It assumes independent measurement errors across entered rows and times, with prediction measurements independent of calibration. Selecting a different CV scheme does not introduce a correlated-error model.

Normalization rescales the response errors using the selected transformation, with reference values treated as fixed. It does not propagate the joint uncertainty involved in estimating a normalization reference from the same observations. Normalizing a response to 1 therefore does not make its supplied SD/SEM zero. These are conditional assumptions, not evidence that normalization references or measurements are exact.


\subsection{Model comparability before interpreting metrics}

Performance metrics are most meaningful when the models being compared were evaluated under identical conditions. Comparisons should use the same observations, preprocessing, analyzed output scale, selected IVIVC approach, and source-specific uncertainty assumptions.

In particular, do not compare AIC, AIC\textsubscript{c}, or BIC across runs with different data, response transformations, or entered SD/SEM. These criteria describe relative support within a comparable candidate set, not absolute model quality. Approach 3 uses direct mappings rather than a parametric likelihood fit and does not have the same information-criterion comparison.

\subsection{Interpreting goodness of fit and prediction error}

Goodness-of-fit metrics---\(R^2\), adjusted \(R^2\), MSE, MAE, RMSE, and NRMSE---describe how well the model fits the data used to estimate its parameters. Their interpretation depends on practical context, not just numerical value. The app does not apply a universal IVIVC performance cutoff; interpretation depends on the intended use and, for regulatory contexts, applicable FDA guidance \cite{fda_modeling_guidance,fda_reporting_guidance}.

Strong goodness-of-fit can still be misleading, particularly for small datasets or models with many parameters. Favorable fit metrics do not rule out unstable parameters, poor cross-validation performance, systematic residual patterns, or a fitted curve shape that is not scientifically plausible for the degradation process being modeled.

The ordinary descriptive metrics remain unweighted when SD/SEM is supplied. A weighted fit can therefore have a larger ordinary RMSE than an unweighted fit while better matching observations with smaller supplied errors. Review these scores alongside the stated fitting objective rather than expecting every metric to improve.

RMSE uses the units of the analyzed fitted output: in vivo time for Approach 1, or response for Approach 2. The two NRMSE options change the denominator rather than incorporating experimental error bars \cite{hyndman2006}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{NRMSE (mean)} asks how large the typical error is relative to the average analyzed output. It is most interpretable for a positive mean away from zero; a negative mean does not give the usual positive relative-error interpretation.
    \item \textbf{NRMSE (std. dev.)} asks how large the typical error is relative to the spread of the observed outputs. That spread includes the changing trajectory across time; it is not the SD/SEM supplied for an individual observation or mean.
\end{itemize}

NRMSE values are not directly comparable across datasets or response scales when the normalization method differs. More broadly, the practical importance of a given error level depends on context: a numerically small error may still matter when the relevant degradation endpoint has narrow variation, while a larger error may be less consequential during exploratory model development when high predictive precision is not central to the analysis.


\subsection{Interpreting relative model evidence}
\label{app:information-criteria}

Relative model evidence tables compare candidate models using information criteria (i.e., AIC, AIC\textsubscript{c}, and BIC), which balance goodness of fit against model complexity. In this context, \textit{empirical support} means that the observed data provide more support for one model than another within the displayed candidate set, based on the selected information criterion. It does not establish that a model is correct, mechanistic, or predictive outside the observed range. Whether the evidence base is sufficient to support a regulatory submission depends on the intended use and applicable FDA guidance \cite{fda_modeling_guidance,fda_reporting_guidance}.

\subsubsection*{How supplied errors enter the criteria}

For a calibration source with supplied errors, the app uses an independent normal observation-error model. At the fitted parameters, its negative twice log likelihood is
\[
D=-2\log L=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\left(\frac{r_i}{\sigma_i}\right)^2
  +\log(2\pi\sigma_i^2)\right],
\qquad \mathrm{AIC}=D+2k,\quad \mathrm{BIC}=D+k\log n.
\]
Here $n$ counts the response rows used in that fit and $k$ counts fitted curve parameters. Replicate counts do not increase $n$, and fixed model coefficients are not included in $k$.

Without supplied errors, the app retains its RSS-based criterion convention, using $n\log(\mathrm{RSS}/n)$ in place of $D$, with parameter-independent constants omitted and a numerical floor near zero RSS. This estimates a common error scale from the residuals. It answers a different error-model question from treating entered SD/SEM as an absolute scale. Raw RSS alone therefore does not describe the supplied-error likelihood. In both paths the app counts curve parameters only; it does not add an estimated error-scale parameter to $k$. These statements document the software's convention.

\subsubsection*{AIC\textsubscript{c}: retained correction and its interpretation}

For either error convention, the app retains
\[
\mathrm{AIC}_{\mathrm{c}}=\mathrm{AIC}+\frac{2k(k+1)}{n-k-1},
\qquad n>k+1.
\]
When that sample-size condition fails, AIC\textsubscript{c} is unavailable even if AIC or BIC can be calculated. A low observation-to-parameter ratio makes small-sample assumptions important; the often-cited $n/k<40$ rule is a regression rule of thumb \cite{burnham2002}, not an automatic reason to prefer this correction for every fit.

Small-sample corrections depend on the statistical setting \cite{hurvich1989}. Known and estimated error variances are different settings, as illustrated for normal linear regression by Maier's analysis (a preprint) \cite{maier2013}. The app keeps conventional AIC\textsubscript{c} available with supplied SD/SEM, but does not claim that it is an exact small-sample correction for every nonlinear model and supplied-error setting. Interpret it alongside the other selected criteria and model diagnostics, using the same uncertainty assumptions across candidates.

\subsubsection*{Separate and combined Approach 2 support}

Approach 2 reports in vitro support, in vivo support, and a combined summary. The combined value for each criterion is the sum of its two source-specific values, under the independent-source fitting assumptions. In particular, combined AIC\textsubscript{c} adds the two separate corrections and requires both to be defined. It is not a new correction calculated from pooled $n$ and $k$; combined BIC likewise adds the separate BIC values.

One source may use supplied errors while the other estimates its scale from residuals. That mixed configuration remains available, provided each source's observations and error assumptions are kept the same across the compared models. Separate and combined support can differ because a model may describe one source more strongly than the other.

\subsubsection*{Rules of thumb for delta values}

Delta is the difference between a model's criterion value and the lowest value in the candidate set. Common interpretive conventions are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{AIC / AIC\textsubscript{c}}: delta 0--2 suggests similar support; 4--7 suggests less support; greater than 10 suggests essentially no support relative to the leading model \cite{burnham2002}.
    \item \textbf{BIC}: delta 0--2 suggests similar support; 2--6 suggests less support; 6--10 suggests substantially less support; greater than 10 suggests essentially no support relative to the leading model \cite{kass1995}.
\end{itemize}

These are interpretive conventions, not regulatory acceptance criteria.

\subsubsection*{Evidence weights}

For a given criterion, the evidence weight for model $m$ is:
\begin{equation}
W_m = \frac{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\Delta_m\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{J} \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\Delta_j\right)}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta_m$ is the delta value for model $m$ and $J$ is the number of candidate models. Larger weights indicate stronger relative support within the displayed set \cite{burnham2002, wagenmakers2004}. These model-support weights differ from the observation weights $1/\sigma_i^2$ used during fitting and are not absolute probabilities that a model is correct.

\subsubsection*{When multiple models have similar support}

If several models have similar delta values or weights, additional considerations can include model simplicity, parameter plausibility, residual behavior, cross-validation performance, and scientific interpretability.


\subsection{Interpreting cross-validation performance}

Cross-validation evaluates prediction performance using observations left out when the model was fit. In this app, it is most useful as a \textbf{sensitivity diagnostic}: when the cross-validation error is similar to the error from fitting the full dataset, this suggests the fitted relationship is reasonably stable regardless of which observations are set aside. When the two differ substantially, the model may be more sensitive to which specific data points were included in fitting, or to the particular way the data were split. Cross-validation does not measure uncertainty in the model parameters. The practical question to keep in mind is not just ``how good is the score?'' but also ``what kind of prediction task did the cross-validation scheme actually create?''

\subsubsection*{What each scheme is testing}

Different schemes create different prediction tasks. Shuffle split, the default, sets aside randomly selected observation rows and checks overall curve-shape robustness. Leave-one-timepoint-out omits one row at a time; observations sharing a time are not grouped together. Leave-contiguous-block-out omits adjacent rows in fitting-x order and checks sensitivity to a missing region. These checks can be gentle when nearby observations carry similar information.

Preprocessing and alignment are established before the splits, rather than rebuilt using each training subset alone. Starting values can also come from the full-data initialization search. The results therefore describe sensitivity within the chosen analysis setup; they are not independent validation of the complete analysis procedure.

These distinctions matter because adjacent time points in a degradation profile are often similar to one another, measurement errors may be related across time, and early, middle, and late portions of a degradation profile may reflect qualitatively different behavior. Standard cross-validation procedures are reviewed in \cite{arlot2010}; additional considerations for temporally ordered or dependent data are discussed in \cite{bergmeir2012,roberts2017,bergmeir2018}.

\subsubsection*{Interpreting results for small IVIVC datasets}

For the small datasets typical in IVIVC development, cross-validation results are intended as an exploratory diagnostic or sensitivity analysis rather than a definitive performance benchmark. Interpretation can include comparison of the CV metric with the corresponding metric for the model fitted to the full dataset together with whether the chosen splitting pattern reflects the practical prediction question of interest.

For example, strong leave-one-timepoint-out performance alongside weaker leave-contiguous-block-out performance may indicate that the model can interpolate individual missing points well, but becomes less reliable when an entire local region of the profile is absent from fitting. The importance of that distinction depends on the intended use of the model. For credibility assessment in the context of regulatory submissions, see FDA guidance on computational modeling credibility and reporting \cite{fda_modeling_guidance,fda_reporting_guidance}.

\subsubsection*{CV fit variability plots}

Average CV metrics summarize prediction error across the observations set aside and the CV splits, but do not show how much the fitted curve itself changes between training subsets. Two models can have similar average CV errors while differing in sensitivity to omitted observations.

The optional CV fit variability plot provides this complementary information by showing the full-data fit together with the mean fitted curve and mean $\pm$ one standard deviation across the successful CV training-set fits. A narrow band indicates that the fitted relationship changes relatively little across the CV splits. A wider band, or widening confined to a particular part of the curve, identifies regions where the fitted relationship is more sensitive to the training data, suggesting that additional data in that region may be particularly informative. A noticeable difference between the mean CV fit and the full-data fit can likewise indicate that repeatedly leaving out observations changes the fitted relationship in a systematic way.

The band is a descriptive sensitivity diagnostic, not a confidence interval, prediction interval, or parameter-uncertainty interval. Its appearance depends on the selected CV scheme and the number of successful CV fits; interpretation therefore also depends on the average CV metrics and the prediction task represented by the chosen splitting scheme.

\subsubsection*{Fit/CV ratios}

The displayed ratio is the full-fit value divided by the mean CV value. For a positive error metric such as RMSE, worse CV error gives a ratio below 1. For positive $R^2$ values, a smaller CV score gives a ratio above 1. Ratios can be undefined or difficult to interpret near zero or with negative scores. Review the raw values, splitting scheme, and fitted plots; a ratio alone does not establish generalizability or overfitting.

\subsubsection*{Cross-validation comparison badges}

The app displays cross-validation comparison badges as interpretive aids. Badges are assigned based on the preferred direction of the metric: for lower-is-better metrics such as RMSE, a worse CV result means the CV value is larger than the full-fit value; for higher-is-better metrics such as $R^2$, a worse CV result means the CV value is smaller. The thresholds used are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Similar}: CV performance is no more than 1.25-fold worse than the full-fit value, or is better under that metric.
    \item \textbf{Moderately worse}: CV performance is 1.25- to 2-fold worse than the full-fit value.
    \item \textbf{Substantially worse}: CV performance is more than 2-fold worse than the full-fit value.
\end{itemize}

The badges use a direction-aware worsening factor, rather than applying these thresholds directly to the displayed fit/CV ratio. Undefined factors produce an unavailable badge. These thresholds are heuristic review aids, not statistical acceptance criteria or regulatory standards.

\subsubsection*{Additional CV summaries with supplied SD/SEM}

Each Approach 2 CV training fit uses the supplied errors for its training rows. The additional supplied-error summary compares predictions with the errors assigned to the omitted observations. If $z_a$ is the omitted prediction error divided by that observation's SD/SEM, the pooled score is
\[
\mathrm{RMSE}_{\mathrm{standardized}}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{M}\sum_{a=1}^{M}z_a^2}.
\]
Here $M$ counts test-observation occurrences across splits: a row used as test data repeatedly is counted repeatedly. It is not a count of additional independent measurements. A score near 1 means the omitted errors are comparable to the supplied SD/SEM scale on average, not that the model passes a calibrated test.

The observation negative log likelihood uses the supplied normal error model evaluated at each CV fitted prediction; the report also gives its mean per test occurrence. It assesses the fitted prediction against that measurement-error model without adding uncertainty from estimating the training-fit parameters. Compare values only for comparable test data, error assumptions, and units; absolute values are not model-quality grades.

These pooled supplied-error scores require a finite score from every requested split. They are unavailable if CV is disabled or the requested splits are incomplete. Ordinary CV means can still summarize finite split scores, so inspect the successful-split and finite-score counts before interpreting them. Supplied-error CV information criteria are not calculated; full-fit AIC, AIC\textsubscript{c}, and BIC remain available.

\subsubsection*{Metrics excluded from cross-validation comparisons}

The app does not give fit/CV comparisons for adjusted $R^2$, information criteria (AIC, AIC\textsubscript{c}, and BIC), or either NRMSE normalization. These quantities depend on the sample size or subset-dependent reference values:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Adjusted $R^2$ and information criteria} incorporate the number of observations or fitted parameters as part of their calculation. Because the full-data model and each cross-validation model are fitted to different subsets of the data, these quantities are not on the same scale and are not directly comparable.
    \item \textbf{NRMSE} is normalized by a factor that generally differs across subsets. This means the full-fit and CV values can have different denominators, making a direct comparison misleading.
\end{itemize}

Eligible cross-validation metrics summarize prediction behavior during CV, while the ineligible CV metrics remain available for evaluating and comparing fitted models. Not all eligible metrics are available for every cross-validation scheme because some require more than one observation to be defined or stable. For example, \(R^2\) cannot be calculated for a single omitted point and will be unavailable under leave-one-timepoint-out CV; interpretation then depends on the metrics that are reported.


\subsection{Interpreting residual diagnostics}

Residuals are observed minus fitted outputs. They use analyzed in vivo time units for Approach 1 and analyzed response units for Approach 2. The app provides raw residual and normal quantile-quantile (Q-Q) plots to explore patterns \cite{kutner2005}. With few observations, random variation can resemble structure, so these are exploratory aids rather than pass/fail tests.

\subsubsection*{Residual-vs-time plots}

Raw residuals retain the units of the analyzed fitted output. Each plot has its own symmetric axis and a minimum display range so tiny numerical errors are not magnified. Use the axes and ordinary error metrics when comparing magnitudes across fits. With unequal supplied SD/SEM, unequal raw residual spread can be consistent with the entered error model; raw residuals are not divided by those errors.

\subsubsection*{Q-Q plots}

The Q-Q display centers residuals and divides them by their sample standard deviation before comparison with a normal reference. It does not standardize each residual by its supplied SD/SEM. With unequal measurement errors, the pooled residual distribution need not look normal even when individual errors follow the supplied normal model. Curvature, tail departures, or isolated points prompt review; a straight pattern does not establish independence, model correctness, or agreement with the supplied error scale. If residual variation is too small, the app reports that the display is not informative.

\subsubsection*{What to look for}

Residual diagnostics are intended for qualitative review---by eye---rather than use as formal statistical tests. Quantitative tests are available for problems such as non-random errors, correlated errors across time (autocorrelation), and variance that changes across the response range (heteroscedasticity) \cite{kutner2005}. However, with the small numbers of observations typical of IVIVC datasets, these tests may have limited ability to distinguish systematic problems from random variation. Visual patterns are prompts for further review rather than definitive findings. Residuals scattered around zero in the residual-vs-time plot, and points near the diagonal in the Q-Q plot, are consistent with a model form that captures the observed pattern, though they do not establish model validity. The following patterns may indicate bias or a model form that does not fully capture the data:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Sustained curvature or systematic trends in the residuals over time.
    \item Clustering, where residuals at adjacent time points are consistently on the same side of zero.
\end{itemize}

When any of these patterns appear, they can motivate evaluation of whether a different model form or a transformed (i.e., normalized or scaled) response better describes the underlying relationship. For a visual example and additional references for interpreting residual plots, see Appendix~\ref{app:model-comparison-tutorial}. 


\subsection{Interpreting parameter uncertainty}

Model parameters cannot always be estimated precisely. Where available, the app reports each estimate $\pm$ the half-width of an approximate 95\% confidence interval. The parameter standard error is the square root of its covariance diagonal entry; it is not the SD/SEM supplied for an observation. A wide coefficient interval indicates imprecision under the fitted model and error assumptions. A narrow interval is conditional on those assumptions and does not establish model correctness.

With supplied absolute errors, the coefficient interval uses a normal multiplier (approximately 1.96 at 95\%), conditional on those errors. Without supplied errors, the app uses a Student $t$ multiplier based on residual degrees of freedom $n-k$ when available, with its normal fallback otherwise. Replicate counts do not supply extra degrees of freedom or choose a different multiplier.

The covariance is a local approximation around the fitted parameters; nonlinear curvature or weakly determined parameters can make that approximation inaccurate \cite{scipyCurveFit}. Intervals can then understate asymmetry or dependence. Review them alongside parameter coupling, the fitted curves, and scientific plausibility \cite{motulsky1987,bates2007}.

The app summarizes parameter uncertainty using informational badges as screening aids, not acceptance criteria. They highlight fitted-parameter uncertainty that may affect interpretation or prediction.

Strong fit metrics alone may not be sufficient to address concerns about poorly estimated parameters or scientifically implausible values; these aspects warrant separate review.

\subsection{How curve and prediction intervals are calculated}
\label{app:uncertainty-calculation}

For fitted curves and parametric predictions, the app samples complete parameter vectors from a multivariate normal covariance approximation. This preserves estimated parameter relationships within a fit and dependence across times along each sampled curve. Separate calibration fits are sampled independently. When prediction SD/SEM is supplied, independent normal response errors are propagated once per entered row; inverse mapping uses the same sampled response to calculate its time and response coordinate.

Sampled bounds enclose the middle 95\% of calculated values at each position. They are marginal, pointwise intervals, not a simultaneous whole-curve band or joint time-response region. Without future variability, time-constant and inverse response-axis bounds come directly from the supplied normal input error model. Selecting future variability adds independent normal future measurement noise to the propagated response draws; inverse future-response bounds use the same successful mappings as the time bounds. See Section~\ref{sec:uncertainty} for the display legend and method-specific interpretation.

Central estimates use the fitted parameters and analyzed inputs, not averages across draws. Other than inverse time intervals and their optional future-response intervals, sampled intervals require every requested calculation to succeed at the affected position. Warnings or invalid results make the time constant unavailable without discarding the fitted curve. Inverse mapping starts in the calibration time span and can extend it by doubling, with a current default of up to three extensions. Unscaled time has a lower search bound of zero, or the observed minimum if already negative; log-time coordinates have no zero-coordinate floor. The limit used is recorded with the result. The finite search and numerical monotonicity checks examine a branch of the fitted curve; they do not prove global uniqueness or that no solution exists outside the examined range.


\subsection{Considering model complexity}

More complex models (i.e., those with more parameters) may fit observed data more closely, but with small datasets this may be a liability rather than an asset. A flexible model may trace the noise in the data rather than the underlying pattern, and its parameters may be poorly estimated as a result, an issue called \textit{overfitting}. Adjusted \(R^2\), AIC, AIC\textsubscript{c}, BIC, cross-validation metrics, and residual diagnostics provide complementary information about whether added complexity is justified \cite{heinze2018}.

When two models have similar fit and similar empirical support, model simplicity and scientific plausibility are among the factors that may support the rationale for the selected model.


\subsection{Scientific plausibility and intended use}

Statistical performance alone is not sufficient to evaluate a model. Scientific plausibility also depends on what is known about the material, degradation mechanism, and test conditions. A model that is statistically competitive but produces a fitted shape, parameter values, or extrapolated behavior that conflicts with known degradation behavior may warrant additional rationale regardless of its metrics.

The intended use of the model---including whether it will inform a regulatory submission---affects the type and depth of supporting evidence that may be relevant. Applicable considerations are described in FDA guidance on computational modeling credibility and reporting \cite{fda_modeling_guidance,fda_reporting_guidance}.


\subsection{Example model review workflow}

The following is one possible sequence of review steps for illustrative reference. It does not establish a required procedure or replace applicable credibility and submission guidance \cite{fda_modeling_guidance,fda_reporting_guidance}.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Confirm that the dataset, preprocessing choices, and selected response values (the measured values being modeled) are appropriate for the intended analysis.
    \item Review fitted curves and residual plots for each candidate model, checking for systematic patterns or implausible fitted shapes.
    \item Compare goodness-of-fit metrics such as \(R^2\) and RMSE in the context of assay variability, endpoint importance, and intended use.
    \item Review cross-validation performance and fit/CV ratios to assess sensitivity to the available data.
    \item Review relative model evidence using information criteria to compare fit quality against model complexity.
    \item Review fitted-parameter estimates, 95\% confidence intervals, uncertainty diagnostics, and scientific plausibility.
    \item Consider whether model complexity is justified by the available data and the degradation mechanism.
    \item Document the rationale for the user-selected model based on a balance of statistical performance, scientific plausibility, and intended use.
\end{enumerate}


\subsection{Questions to guide model review}

The following questions illustrate considerations that a user might explore. They do not define a required review process or imply that each question must be addressed in a regulatory submission.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Does the model describe the observed data well?}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Does the fitted curve follow the main trend in the data?
        \item How do performance metrics such as $R^2$, RMSE, and NRMSE compare in the context of the intended use?
        \item How large is the prediction error relative to assay variability, measurement uncertainty, or the degradation endpoint being evaluated?
        \item Are there regions where the model consistently overpredicts or underpredicts?
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Does the model generalize beyond the fitted data?}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Are cross-validation metrics similar to the metrics for the model fitted to the full dataset, and are fit/CV ratios near 1?
        \item Could the cross-validation result be sensitive to the splitting scheme, number of observations, or a small number of influential points?
        \item If cross-validation performance is poor, how does that affect interpretation of the model for its intended purpose, including whether a simpler or more stable form may warrant consideration?
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Are the model parameters interpretable and stable?}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Are the fitted parameter values scientifically plausible?
        \item What do the 95\% confidence intervals indicate about the precision of the parameter estimates?
        \item Are any parameters strongly coupled---meaning different combinations of parameter values could produce a similar fitted curve?
        \item If coupling is present, does it coincide with large parameter uncertainty or an identifiability caution?
        \item Are individual parameter values being interpreted mechanistically, or is the model being used mainly to describe the overall curve shape?
    \end{itemize}

    A model can have a fitted curve that closely follows the observed data while still having uncertain or coupled parameters. In that case, the curve may be useful for empirical description within the observed data range. Interpret individual parameter values cautiously.

    \item \textbf{Is the model unnecessarily complex?}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Does the more complex model improve fit, cross-validation behavior, or relative model evidence enough to support the added parameters?
        \item Do AIC, AIC\textsubscript{c}, or BIC provide support for the additional complexity within the candidate model set?
        \item If two models perform similarly, how do model simplicity and scientific plausibility contribute to the rationale for the selected model?
        \item Does the dataset contain enough observations to inform the fitted parameters?
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Is the model scientifically plausible?}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Is the fitted curve shape consistent with expected material degradation behavior?
        \item How do the model assumptions align with the material, test conditions, and degradation endpoint?
        \item How does the model behave near the edges of the observed data range, and how would that behavior affect interpolation or extrapolation?
    \end{itemize}

    A statistically competitive model may still warrant additional rationale if its shape, parameter values, or extrapolated behavior conflict with the known degradation process.

    \item \textbf{How does the intended use affect interpretation of the model?}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Is the model being used for exploratory analysis, interpolation, prediction, comparison across conditions, or support for a regulatory rationale?
        \item How does the level of uncertainty affect interpretation for that purpose?
        \item Are the model's limitations clearly documented?
        \item Would an independent reviewer understand the rationale for selecting this model over other candidate models?
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

Potential elements of a model-selection rationale include fitted performance, cross-validation behavior, relative empirical support, parameter diagnostics, residual behavior, scientific plausibility, and intended use.


\subsection{Example interpretation statements}

The following passages illustrate how model results might be described in a technical context. They are examples only and are not FDA-endorsed language for regulatory submissions. Adapt all language to the specific analysis and supporting evidence.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Model A had the lowest RMSE and highest adjusted $R^2$ among the evaluated models, indicating the strongest goodness-of-fit under the selected preprocessing conditions.
    \item Model A was selected over the more complex Model B because both models showed similar fit and AIC\textsubscript{c} support (Delta AIC\textsubscript{c} $<2$), so the observed data provided little additional support for the added complexity of Model B.
    \item Although Model C had strong goodness-of-fit, its cross-validation error was substantially higher than its fitted error, suggesting sensitivity to which observations were included in fitting.
    \item Residuals for Model A were scattered around zero without obvious systematic patterns, consistent with a model form that captures the observed data.
    \item Model B showed a systematic trend in the residuals across time, suggesting the selected model form does not fully capture the degradation profile.
    \item The fitted parameters for Model A were within plausible ranges for the degradation mechanism, with narrow local confidence intervals under the selected error assumptions.
    \item Model C showed strong parameter coupling between the rate and shape parameters, with relative standard errors exceeding 100\%. Parameter estimates were retained for curve description but were not interpreted individually.
    \item The selected model was chosen based on a balance of goodness-of-fit, cross-validation behavior, relative empirical support, parameter stability, and scientific plausibility for the degradation mechanism and intended use.
\end{itemize}
